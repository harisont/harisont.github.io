## 1. Travel light
When it comes to long-distance travel, my first and most obvious piece of advice is to try to pack as little as possible.
If you can carry one, the best option is probably a hiking backpack.
In general, luggage you can comfortably walk around with allows you to make the most of waits between trains, even when they're not long enough to make the luggage deposit worth its price. 

## 2. Don't forget the essentials
Packing light is all good and well as long as you don't underpack.
What's essential is highly subjective: many would say power banks and sleep masks are a must, but I usually carry neither.

Things I find useful while on a train include water, a renewable source of literature (aka an e-reading device), _both_ headphones _and_ earplugs (the baseline noise of some trains can be exhausting even in a quiet compartment and I don't like active noise cancelling) and hand sanitizer, since train toilets often lack soap even on the best lines.

Once I get off the train, I usually want a smaller bag to use if I leave my luggage at the station (ideally a foldable backpack, but a totebag can also do the job), a raincoat or umbrella, basic cutlery for supermarket food and some hostel essentials (padlock, flip-flops, towel).

## 3. Carefully choose your clothes
Besides these items, most of what I pack consists of clothes.
During long-distance trips, it is important to me to feel comfortable in my body. 
In practice, that means layers (regardless of the season) and wide-leg/stretchy trousers, sometimes even skirts.

Another thing I always try (but fail, because I was born female) to have is pockets. 
Loosing my documents during an international trip is something I'd rather never experience, so I prefer to always have them on me.

Throughout the summer, I also always carry a bikini, even if no swimming is planned. 
Unplanned bathing is the best bathing, it takes up very little space and can serve as extra underwear.

## 4. Prepare to be offline most of the time
Despite more and more train offering WLANs, internet access during travel should not be taken for granted.
Even if you rely on your own mobile data, the mountainous coutries like Austria and Italy will give you a hard time.
Plus, while EU roaming is a great thing, I've experienced way too many days-long connection failures due to mystery misconfigurations to fully trust it.

So, when travelling across several countries, I like to assume I may be offline most of the time (although I also expect to be to briefly connect to a Wi-Fi once at a major train station. This it likely, but also not a given).
For me, this implies some "digital packing".
In descending order of importance, I usually try to prepare:

- tickets, seats reservations etc.
- maps of the places I'm planning to stop at. For this purpose, I rely on CoMaps, a free and open source OpenStreetMap-based app
- music. I've recently set up my own music server, from which I download a few albus before each trip, trying to anticipate what I'll want to listen to
- podcasts. I always have a few pre-downloaded on my favorite client, AntennaPod (also FOSS)
- e-books (unless I'm happy with a physical book).

## 5. Always consider a rail pass
You might think passes like the Interrail ones are expensive, and you'd be very right.
Long-distance travel _without_ a rail pass, however, tends to be a _lot_ more expensive.

## Bonus tips for fellow Scanditalians
If you have the (mis)fortune of routinely crossing Europe north-to-south like I do, here's some extra advice for you:

- __the Danes really like passport checks__. Remeber that free borders Eutopia? Between Sweden and Denmark, as well as between Denmark and Germany, this is indeed still a utopia. If you have any potential visa issues, this is definitely something to consider
- __the central station's luggage deposit in Copenaghen has a fixed daily price__. Las time I was there, it was 70 Danish crowns for a smaller cabinet, 95 for a larger one
- __the night train to Sicily has some quirks__. The Termini-Termini (Roma Termini to Termini Imerese) Intercity Notte is a complicated train. It divides up and, in Villa S. Giovanni, it boards a ferry to Messina. To avoid any surprises when you wake up, I strongly recommend triple checking that you're sitting in the correct compartment for your destination. As for reliability, I have been a passenger on the this train several times now, and the experience is always the same: halfway through the trip, it seems to be catastrophically delayed, but always manages to catch up in the end. I wouldn't count on catching connections from the intermediate stops, but if your next train is from Termini Imerese, that might be fine. In any case, I wouldn't waste too much time staring at the train status on the Trenitalia app: once it reaches Sicily, the train invariably changes name and figuring out the new one is a nontrivial task
- __if you're travelling to Sicily by day__, keep in mind that __it is much faster to get off the train in Villa S. Giovanni and board the ferry to Messina on foot__. As far as I remember, this ferry is not included in Interrail passes, but it isn't very expensive. If your time is money, it may be a good option  
- __Italian high-speed trains (Frecciarossa) are comfortable, but they come with hidden costs__. Last time I checked, the Interrail app offered mandatory seat reservations for no less than 15€
- __refilling your water bottle is done differently in different countries__. In Sweden, it is perfectly acceptable to enter a cafe and ask for some tap water. In Italy, this is less customary (you can still ask, but keep in mind that drinking water is not as easy to come by in the first place, especially in the south), but water fountains are much more common.
