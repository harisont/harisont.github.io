After moving to Sweden in 2018, I found myself traveling longer distances on a regular basis for the first time to visit friends and family both in my hometown, Perugia, and in Agrigento, where most of my relatives live.
At the same time, I became more aware of the impact of airplane travel on the climate.
After a couple years of back-and-forth flights, a [_Stay grounded_](https://stay-grounded.org/lets-stay-grounded/travel-differently/) campaign allowed me to test out long-distance train travel, something I'd always been attracted to despite its prohibitive prices for a student.
Many train rides followed that first three-day trip, most of which along the same approximate route from Sweden through Denmark Germany, Austria (or Switzerland) to Italy (or vice versa):

![Maps of five different Interrail trips, all somewhere between southern Sweden and Italy](../assets/img/ttttt.jpg)

I'm not always able to travel by train, let alone the whole way, but I try to ar least consider it whenever I'm not too short on time.
Not only is train travel more environmentally friendly than flying, but it can also be more pleasant. 
Slower travel reminds of the distance that, for better of worse, I've put between myself and some of my closest people, it works as a much needed buffer between a tough semester and a series of family gatherings and it gives me an excuse to visit new places along the way.

On the other hand, crossing Europe by train isn't something I want to romanticize. 
During my last trip back to Sweden from Italy, a series of cancellations, delays leading to missed connections and innumerable smaller inconveniences made the whole trip so frustrating that, if it wasn't for a couple nice conversations in Hamburg and a sunny couple hours in Copenaghen, I'm not sure there'd be a next one.
Unfortunately, this is not an uncommon experience.
As I explained [in this thread](https://mstdn.social/@harisont/117016139549596536), I feel less and less encouraged to choose rails over air: prices are crazy, bookings complicated, infrastructure unreliable.

If you're new to cross-border train travel and still wanna give it a try, here are a few tips to make your trip a little less exhausting and hopefully have some fun on the way.
If you already travel by train but want some advice about my usual route, you can jump directly to my [bonus tips for fellow Scanditalians](#bonus-tips-for-fellow-scanditalians).

## 1. Travel light
When it comes to long-distance travel, my first and most obvious piece of advice is to try to pack as little as possible.
Unless you have issues with your back, the best option is probably a single hiking backpack.
In general, luggage you can comfortably walk around with allows you to make the most of waits between trains, even when they're not long enough to make the luggage deposit worth it. 

## 2. Don't forget the essentials
Packing light is all good and well as long as you don't underpack.
What's essential is highly subjective: many would say power banks and sleep masks are a must, but I usually carry neither.

Things I find useful while on a train include water, a renewable source of literature (aka an e-reading device), _both_ headphones _and_ earplugs (the baseline noise of some trains can be exhausting even in a quiet compartment and I have beef with ANC) and hand sanitizer, since even toilets even on the best lines often lack soap.

Once I get off the train, I usually want a smaller bag to use if I leave my luggage at the station (ideally a foldable backpack, but a totebag can also do the job), a raincoat or umbrella, basic cutlery for supermarket food and possibly some hostel essentials (padlock, flip-flops, towel).

## 3. Carefully choose your clothes
Besides these items, most of what I pack consists of clothing.
During long-distance trips, it is important to me to feel comfortable in my body. 
In practice, that means layers (regardless of the season) and wide-leg/stretchy trousers, sometimes even skirts.

Another thing I always try to have (and often fail, because I was born female) is pockets. 
Loosing my documents during an international trip is something I've never experienced and I'd like to keep it that way, so I prefer to always have them on me.

Throughout the summer, I also always carry a bikini, even if no bathing is planned. 
Unplanned swims are the best swims, it takes up very little space and can serve as extra underwear.

## 4. Prepare to be offline most of the time
Despite more and more train offering WLANs (sometimes remarkably good), internet access during travel should not be taken for granted.
Even if you rely on your own mobile data, mountainous countries like Austria and Italy will give you a hard time.
Plus, while EU roaming is a great thing, I've experienced way too many days-long connection failures due to mystery misconfigurations to fully trust it.

So, when traveling across several countries, I prefer to assume I will be offline most of the time (although I also expect to be to briefly connect to a Wi-Fi once at a major train station. This it likely, but also not a given).
For me, this implies some "digital packing".
In descending order of importance, I usually try to prepare:

- tickets, seats reservations etc.
- maps of the places I'm planning to stop at. For this purpose, I rely on CoMaps, a free and open source OpenStreetMap-based app
- music and podcasts
- e-books (unless I'm happy with a long physical book).

## 5. Always consider a rail pass
You might think passes like the Interrail ones are expensive, and you'd be very right.
Long-distance travel _without_ a rail pass, however, tends to be a _lot_ more expensive.
Even in cases where booking trains individually seems cheaper, I strongly recommend considering a rail pass.
In the event of a significant delay (which you should expect in every long train trip), it's usually easy (at least in some countries) to get a reimbursement for that specific train, but nobody will pay you back for any missed connections.
Traveling with a pass gives you the peace of mind that, if you miss a train, you can always get the next one without additional costs (modulo [seat reservations](#6-minimize-seat-reservations)).

## 6. Minimize seat reservations
Unfortunately, traveling with a rail pass is not as flexible as it looks.
A _lot_ of European force seat reservations, which come with additional fees. 
In my personal experience, these are between 3 and 15€ depending on where you book (yes, different sites have very different prices) and where you go. 
While this might not sound like a lot, seat reservations quickly add up.
For that reason, my advice is to avoid these reservations as much as possible, even if that means taking a regional train instead of a high-speed train.
Sometimes this is not possible, in which case it's up to you to decide whether to prebook your train, risking having to pay the fee twice in case of a missed connection, or wait until the very last minute, risking not to find a seat.
I usually opt for the latter, and in more than one occasion I managed to convince the train personell to let me in anyway: it's almost impossible for all seats to be occupied for the entire duration of the trip. 

There are two important exceptions to this rule: night trains and the first leg of you trip. 
If you want to sleep (horizontally) on a night train, you should definitely book well in advance. 
If you have the chance to start a long trip with a high-speed train and the route is popular, a reservation might be worth considering.

## 7. Keep track of places to (re)visit
On top of expecting setbacks, you should try to embrace the setbacks.
Missed your connection in Hamburg? 
Well, there's a museum to visit right next to the train station!
Stranded in Bologna? 
What's a good hostel to spend the night?

In some cases, you'll have to improvise, but it's good to keep a set of possible alternative stops in mind when planning a trip.
This gets easier the more you travel in the same areas. 
When you travel the same route over and over like me, it can also be a good idea to take note of places to revisit (or to avoid).
In my maps app, I have pinned a few strategically located places I want to visit. 
Recently, I've also started using it to note down the best and worst overnight stays.

## 8. Be aware that not all schedules are created equal
You would think that the schedule on your phone is the same as the departures board at the train station, and you'd be right _most of the time_.
Actually, only some of the time depending on what schedule you're looking at on your phone.

Based on my personal experience, a good rule of thumb is that the Interrail app is less reliable than the local railway website/app, which is in turn less reliable than the station's departure board (which is still less reliable than the spoken announcements, but whether the announcements are comprehensible depends on you language skills, hearing and most importantly on the acoustics of the place).

In particular, remember that the Interrail schedule is _not_ updated in real time, or at least that the schedule you see when you are offline is not necessarily the same schedule you saw when you were online last time. 
It might very well be older. 

## 9. Talk to people
My second to last piece of advice is to try not to be afraid of talking to people, and I mean it in two different ways:

1. absolutely, definitely talk to the ticket inspector if you know you're breaking a rule for lack of a better option (e.g. if you've boarded the train without a mandatory seat reservation because the site was down, the train was fully booked or you really didn't make it in time). If you speak for yourself, chances are you won't be fined. Similarly, ask for help if you get stuck at a train station. The fact that (an unreliable version of) the schedule is in your pocket doesn't make info points useless. People working there may have crucial bits of last-minute information and sometimes they can be quite empathetic
2. if you're having a frustrating ride, try to exchange a couple words with your neighbor. If you end up staying at a hostel, see if you can take it as a chance to socialize. Sometimes these random exchanges are what makes travel interesting, or at least bearable.

## Bonus tips for fellow Scanditalians
If you have the (mis)fortune of routinely crossing Europe north-to-south like I do, here's some extra advice for you:

- __the Danes really like passport checks__. Remeber that free borders Eutopia? Between Sweden and Denmark, as well as between Denmark and Germany, this is indeed still a utopia. If you have any potential visa issues, this is definitely something to consider
- __the central station's luggage deposit in Copenaghen has a fixed daily price__. Last time I was there, it was 70 Danish crowns for a smaller cabinet, 95 for a larger one
- __some trains between Austria and Italy require an additional fee__, but you can pay it right on the train, and if they don't ask about it, you don't need to remind them, even if that contradicts part of point 9. Traveling by train is already expensive enough
- __the night train to Sicily has some quirks__. The Termini-Termini (Roma Termini to Termini Imerese) Intercity Notte is a complicated train. It divides up and, in Villa S. Giovanni, it boards a ferry to Messina. To avoid any surprises when you wake up, I strongly recommend triple checking that you're sitting in the correct compartment for your destination. As for reliability, I have been a passenger on the this train several times now, and the experience is always the same: halfway through the trip, it seems to be catastrophically delayed, but always manages to catch up in the end. I wouldn't count on catching connections from the intermediate stops, but if your next train is from Termini Imerese, that might be fine. In any case, I wouldn't waste too much time staring at the train status on the Trenitalia app: once it reaches Sicily, the train invariably changes name and figuring out the new one is a nontrivial task to say the least
- __if you're travelling to Sicily by day__, keep in mind that __it is much faster to get off the train in Villa S. Giovanni and board the ferry to Messina on foot__. As far as I remember, this ferry is not included in Interrail passes, but it isn't very expensive. If your time is money, it may be a good option
- __Italian high-speed trains (Frecciarossa) are comfortable, but they come with hidden costs__. Last time I checked, the Interrail app offered mandatory seat reservations for no less than 15€
- __refilling your water bottle is done differently in different countries__. In Sweden, it is perfectly acceptable to enter a cafe and ask for some tap water. In Italy, this is less customary (you can still ask, but keep in mind that drinking water is not as easy to come by in the first place, especially in the south), but water fountains are much more common.
